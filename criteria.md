# Promo rating criteria: show continuation

Status: selected approach, version 1. Recorded on 8 November 2026.

## Objective

Help a microdrama editor identify and compare promising 20–60-second promos that make a new viewer stop, understand the situation, care about its outcome, and want to continue into the show.

The selected priority is **show continuation**. Immediate attention remains essential, but the promo must also create interest in this particular story. A striking moment that leaves no reason to follow the characters is less valuable for this objective.

The target for the supplied input pack is a new English-speaking US viewer. Do not assume the viewer already knows the characters, relationships, setting, or plot.

These weights are proposed editorial starting points. They are not Kuku's published scoring system, measured conversion probabilities, or experimentally validated weights.

## What we rate

Rate a **specific proposed promo**, with an exact opening, ending, and ordered source segments. Do not rate an entire episode as though every possible cut has the same quality.

A changed opening or ending requires a fresh assessment. For a promo joining multiple segments, evaluate the assembled sequence, including whether its transitions preserve meaning.

## Qualifying requirements

Check these before ranking. A high weighted score cannot compensate for failing a requirement.

1. **Duration:** the combined selected segments last 20–60 seconds.
2. **Opening hook:** an identifiable hook occurs within the first three seconds of the proposed clip.
3. **Standalone meaning:** a newcomer can understand the basic conflict or situation from the selected material.
4. **Unresolved ending:** the clip leaves a meaningful outcome unresolved, rather than merely stopping at an arbitrary point.
5. **Source fidelity:** the segments exist in the supplied script, the timestamps are traceable, and their arrangement preserves the story's meaning.
6. **Existing material:** the hook and context work without invented dialogue, new narration, music, or text overlays. The assignment asks us to select moments and cut points, not produce an edited ad.

For each requirement, record pass, fail, or needs review. Separate uncertainty about the finished footage from a confirmed problem in the script. Script-based eligibility does not establish that the footage performs as described.

## Weighted rubric

Score each criterion from 0 to 5. Calculate its contribution as `weight × score / 5`; sum the contributions for an editorial score out of 100.

| Criterion | Weight | What a strong candidate demonstrates |
|---|---:|---|
| Opening impact | 20% | An understandable, arresting action, statement, or reveal occurs within three seconds. |
| Emotional pull and investment | 20% | The viewer recognizes a character's desire, vulnerability, dignity, attraction, danger, or potential loss and becomes invested in an outcome. |
| Curiosity created by the opening | 10% | The opening introduces a specific contradiction, secret, surprise, or question that encourages continued attention. |
| Clarity for a new viewer | 15% | The selected material makes the important relationship, conflict, and stakes understandable without prior episodes. |
| Momentum through the clip | 10% | The situation develops through a complication, reaction, escalation, or reversal, sustaining interest after the opening. |
| Ending cliffhanger | 25% | The cut leaves a specific consequential outcome unresolved, giving the viewer a reason to watch the next scene or episode. |
| **Total** | **100%** | |

The ending receives the largest individual weight because the selected objective is turning attention into interest in the show. This does not relax the three-second opening requirement.

## Scoring anchors

Use 0 for absent, 1 for weak, 3 for clear and reasonably compelling, and 5 for exceptionally strong. Use 2 and 4 for intermediate cases. The anchors below make the judgments more consistent; they do not remove subjectivity.

### 1. Opening impact — 20%

Assess only the first three seconds of the proposed cut.

- **0:** No identifiable hook; routine setup or unrelated scenery.
- **1:** A potentially interesting cue appears, but its significance is weak or difficult to recognize quickly.
- **3:** A clear confrontation, surprising statement, arresting action, or reveal establishes an immediate reason to pause.
- **5:** The opening delivers an unusually arresting event or line whose significance is immediately understandable.

Evidence: identify the exact opening action or dialogue and its timestamp. A major event later in the clip must not improve this score.

### 2. Emotional pull and investment — 20%

Assess why the viewer would care about this character or outcome, beyond noticing the event.

- **0:** No discernible emotional connection or meaningful desire or loss.
- **1:** An emotion is visible, but its cause or consequences remain vague.
- **3:** The clip establishes an understandable desire, injustice, attraction, threat, or potential loss.
- **5:** The clip creates a strong, specific emotional investment: the viewer has a clear reason to root for, fear for, or remain fascinated by someone.

Evidence: name the character, what they want or could lose, and the scene evidence supporting that interpretation. Anger, tears, wealth, violence, or a kiss do not automatically establish investment.

### 3. Curiosity created by the opening — 10%

Assess the initial question or contradiction that carries the viewer beyond the hook.

- **0:** The opening creates no meaningful question.
- **1:** Something is unexplained, but the question is generic or the result of missing essential context.
- **3:** The opening introduces a specific secret, surprise, or contradiction the viewer can recognize.
- **5:** The initial question is clear, compelling, and tightly connected to the story's central relationship or conflict.

Evidence: write the opening question in one sentence. Distinguish productive curiosity from confusion about basic facts.

### 4. Clarity for a new viewer — 15%

Assess the selected clip as a whole, while checking that its opening is also understandable.

- **0:** The basic situation cannot be understood without prior episodes.
- **1:** Some actions make sense, but important relationships or stakes remain unclear.
- **3:** The viewer understands the principal relationship, conflict, and potential consequence with little effort.
- **5:** The clip efficiently supplies the context needed to care while leaving the intended mystery unresolved.

Evidence: explain the situation using only information communicated inside the clip. Do not fill gaps using the episode logline, title, continuity notes, or knowledge of later episodes.

### 5. Momentum through the clip — 10%

Assess whether the clip develops after attracting attention.

- **0:** Nothing meaningfully develops.
- **1:** The clip repeats an emotion or explanation with little change.
- **3:** At least one meaningful complication, reaction, escalation, or reversal sustains interest.
- **5:** The sequence progresses efficiently, with each important beat deepening the conflict or changing the viewer's expectations.

Evidence: describe the progression in a few beats. Fast cuts or loud action alone do not establish narrative momentum.

### 6. Ending cliffhanger — 25%

Assess the outcome left unresolved at the proposed cut point.

- **0:** The important conflict is resolved, or the clip simply stops without a meaningful open question.
- **1:** A question remains, but it is arbitrary, trivial, or emotionally weak.
- **3:** A clear, consequential outcome remains unresolved and creates a reason to continue.
- **5:** The ending leaves the viewer strongly invested in a specific imminent decision, confrontation, discovery, or danger that the show can answer.

Evidence: write the ending question in one sentence and identify the exact cut point. For example: “Will the abandoned bride accept the stranger who defended her?”

Opening curiosity and ending suspense are different assessments. The former carries the viewer into the clip; the latter carries them beyond it. Do not automatically award identical scores to both.

## Hook categories: descriptive labels, not bonus points

Label the main appeal and, where useful, a secondary appeal:

- Aspiration and status reversal: wealth, opportunity, influence, recognition, or restored dignity.
- Humiliation and anticipated revenge: an injustice creates a desire to see someone fight back.
- Betrayal and scandal: cheating, deception, exposure, or an intimate secret.
- Hidden identity and unexpected power: a gap between a person's apparent and actual status or abilities.
- Romantic or sexual tension: attraction, an unexpected encounter, a near-kiss, or a forbidden relationship.
- Danger and rescue: a threat to safety or freedom and anticipation of intervention.
- An impossible choice: a character faces competing painful or consequential outcomes.

One scene may combine several categories. Category count does not increase the score. A kiss, billionaire, slap, or scandal receives no automatic advantage.

## Editorial rules for choosing boundaries

- Start close to the meaningful disruption, avoiding unnecessary setup.
- Include enough context to make the disruption matter.
- Allow a small satisfying development when it earns investment, then leave a larger outcome open.
- Cut after the viewer understands the danger, choice, or surprise and before the key outcome is resolved.
- Avoid arbitrary mid-sentence stops whose only effect is withholding basic information.
- Evaluate spoilers by their effect on interest: a reveal can create a bigger question, but it can also give away the show's main satisfaction.
- When combining segments, preserve character relationships, causal meaning, and understandable continuity.

## What to record for every candidate

1. Candidate identifier and one-line description.
2. Episode and start/end timestamps for every segment, in playback order.
3. Combined duration.
4. Qualifying requirement results and any review flags.
5. Main and optional secondary hook category.
6. The opening hook and the question it introduces.
7. The character's desire or possible loss.
8. The ending cliffhanger and specific continuation question.
9. Six criterion scores, each supported by source evidence.
10. Weighted total, concise selection rationale, and important weakness.
11. Separate confidence or footage-review notes.

Confidence is not another weighted criterion. An uncertain visual realization should be visible to the editor rather than hidden inside a precise-looking score.

## Building the shortlist

Rank qualifying candidates using the rubric, then review the shortlist for redundancy and variety. Several almost identical humiliation clips may offer fewer useful choices than strong candidates covering betrayal, rescue, and romance.

Keep individual quality and shortlist variety separate. Any editorial change to posting order should be explained. No numerical acceptance threshold or fixed shortlist size has been validated yet.

## Quality checking

Before applying the rubric across all 50 episodes, review a small, varied set of candidates, including emotionally intense passages that may fail as promos.

Ask unfamiliar reviewers to explain:

- Who matters to them in the clip.
- What that character wants or stands to lose.
- What specifically they want to see happen next.

Record disagreement and bad selections. A resolved triumph, unexplained scandal, repetitive insult, or visually striking moment without story interest can expose weaknesses in the rubric.

With real distribution data, attention measures such as early hold rate would remain useful, but show starts and meaningful continuation into the show would be closer to the selected objective. Clicks alone would not establish that the promo delivered worthwhile viewers. Compare candidates under reasonably comparable conditions and consistent metric definitions.

Until such testing exists, call the output an editorial ranking, not a prediction of virality, conversion, or viewership.
